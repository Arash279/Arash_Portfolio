## The problem

A common-mode choke is the workhorse of an EMI filter. Its impedance depends on the core, the winding and many parasitic effects, and it changes with the number of turns. Designers want to know the curve for every candidate turn count, but measuring each one means winding and testing a physical part.

A published approach uses a neural network to predict unmeasured chokes. I asked a simpler question: **how far can you get with physics and good interpolation alone?**

## The idea: line up the resonance first

Adding turns moves the choke's main resonance to a lower frequency. If you simply average two measured curves point by point, their peaks sit at different frequencies and the result is a smeared, wrong curve.

So the method works in two steps:

1. **Learn how the resonance moves.** Across the reference chokes, resonance frequency follows a power law in turn count, a straight line on a log–log plot:

$$
\ln f_r=a+b\ln N
$$

2. **Shift, then blend.** For a target turn count, take the two nearest measured chokes, shift each curve so its resonance lines up with where the target's should be, then blend log magnitude and phase.

The whole method is a few lines of NumPy, runs instantly and needs no training. One fitted slope carries the physics.

## Results on parts it had never seen

I developed the method on two cores, froze it, then tested it once on a third core (W452): 10 measured reference chokes (2–6 and 46–50 turns) used to predict 39 chokes in between (7–45 turns). The predictions were saved before looking at the true curves.

| Method | Mean error (lower is better) |
| --- | ---: |
| **Resonance-aligned prediction (mine)** | **0.083** |
| Published neural-network predictions (RANN) | 0.095 |
| Blending without alignment | ≈0.46 |
| Simple point-by-point interpolation | ≈0.73 |

*Error is the relative difference between predicted and measured complex impedance across the whole band, averaged over the 39 chokes.*

The aligned method was about 12% better than the neural network on average and better on 27 of the 39 parts. Lining up the resonance is what makes the difference: without it, error is more than five times higher.

## A cheap way to cut error further: measure smarter

The 10 reference chokes in the public dataset are bunched at the two ends of the turn range. Replaying the method with references spread evenly across the range, using existing measurements, cut error by roughly 2–6× in every core and reference-count combination I tried (18 in total).

The engineering takeaway: choosing *which* parts to measure can matter as much as choosing a fancier model.

- Designed and implemented the resonance-aligned predictor in Python/NumPy.

- Compared against the published neural-network predictions part by part and band by band.
- Ran the reference-placement study.

## Limits and next steps

The method needs measured reference chokes on the same core, so it fills in gaps rather than predicting a brand-new design. The neural network still does better above about 20 MHz and on one development core (W620), where higher-order resonances do not follow a single shift. Current work looks at handling those higher-order modes and at deciding automatically when more reference measurements are needed.
