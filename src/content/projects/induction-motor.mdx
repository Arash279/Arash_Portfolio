## Why this matters

Modern drives switch motors with very fast voltage edges. Those edges push high-frequency currents through the motor's stray capacitances, which show up as conducted EMI, bearing currents and insulation stress. To design an EMI filter or check insulation, an engineer needs the motor's impedance not just at 50 Hz but up to tens of megahertz.

The classic motor equivalent circuit stops being useful well below that. The usual alternatives are expensive: finite-element models need detailed geometry and long computation, and black-box or neural-network fits need lots of data and say little about the physics.

**My goal was a model that is cheap to build and cheap to run, yet accurate across the whole band.**

## What makes it practical

| | What it takes |
| --- | --- |
| Input data | One impedance sweep at the motor terminals (1,590 points) |
| Hardware | An ordinary laptop |
| Time to fit | About 20 seconds for a full identification |
| Repeatability | Essentially the same answer on all 10 runs with different random seeds |
| Output | A small circuit (12–14 components) that drops straight into circuit or drive simulation |

Because the result is a circuit, every parameter has a physical meaning: winding resistance and leakage, magnetizing inductance, winding-to-frame capacitance. An engineer can read it, sanity-check it and reuse it, which a black-box curve fit does not allow.

## Exploring the circuit model

I started from a published three-phase motor circuit and developed it in three steps.

1. **11 parameters (starting point).** Captures the main winding, magnetizing and capacitive paths. It follows the low- and mid-frequency response but drifts away at the top of the band.
2. **12 parameters (stable baseline).** Adds an extra inductive branch. This is the version I used for the comparison study below, because it fits well and converges to the same answer every time.
3. **14 parameters (high-frequency extension).** Above about 10 MHz the measured phase rises and the impedance recovers, which no branch in the original circuit can produce. I replaced the extra inductor with a resonant branch, an inductor in parallel with a resistor–capacitor pair:

$$
Z_{\mathrm{lad}}=j\omega L_{\mathrm{ad}}\;\parallel\;\left(R_{\mathrm{ad}}+\frac{1}{j\omega C_{\mathrm{ad}}}\right)
$$

This branch lets the model follow the high-frequency tail in Figure 1. Each step came from looking at where model and measurement disagreed, then asking which physical path could explain the gap.

## Making the fit fast and reliable

Fitting 12–14 coupled parameters whose values span many orders of magnitude, from ohms to kilohms and picofarads to henries, is hard. A naive optimizer gets stuck or gives a different answer every run. Four design choices fixed that:

- **Fit in log space.** Every parameter stays positive, and a picofarad gets the same attention as a henry.
- **Global search, then local polish.** Differential evolution finds the right region; multi-start robust least squares then refines it.
- **Balance the data.** Real and imaginary parts are scaled separately and frequency regions are weighted, so the big resonance peak does not drown out smaller features.
- **Smart starting values.** Capacitances and inductances are first estimated from the flat regions of the measured curve.

## Proof: a head-to-head comparison

To show each design choice earns its place, I ran the 12-parameter fit 10 times with each method and compared the error and the run-to-run spread.

Every simplified version is less accurate, less repeatable, or both. Only the full workflow is accurate *and* gives the same answer every time, which is what an engineer needs before trusting a model in a design.
